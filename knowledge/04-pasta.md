## P1. Salting the cooking water
- **Rule:** use **1 % salt by weight (10 g per litre)** as the default, within a range of **0.5–2 %**. Never "as salty as the sea" (3.5 % is inedible, and even 3 % is too salty). **Drop to 0.5–0.8 %** when the sauce is very salty (pecorino, guanciale, cacio e pepe, carbonara) or when you'll reduce a lot of pasta water into the sauce.
- **Why:** pasta only absorbs salt while it cooks, so under-salted pasta tastes flat whatever the sauce. Pasta water used to finish the sauce concentrates as it reduces.
- **Failure → fix:** if the pasta is bland under a good sauce, the water was under-salted, so weigh the salt. If the dish is too salty, a salty cheese and reduced pasta water were combined with 1–2 % water, so use 0.5 % for cheese-heavy sauces.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats / Daniel Gritzer, "How Salty Should Pasta Water Be?" https://www.seriouseats.com/how-salty-should-pasta-water-be · Serious Eats, "For the Best Pasta in Less Time…" (0.5–2 %) https://www.seriouseats.com/the-best-way-to-boil-pasta-11986269 · Serious Eats / Kenji, "The Right Way to Sauce Pasta" (1–2 %) https://www.seriouseats.com/the-right-way-to-sauce-pasta · NYT Cooking, "Spaghetti Carbonara" (lightly salted, no more than 1 tbsp) https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/12965-spaghetti-carbonara

## P2. Water volume, stirring, no oil
- **Rule:** use **2–3 L water per 450 g dried pasta** (not 4–6 L), in a pot just big enough. A wide 30 cm pan works for long shapes. **Stir during the first 1–2 min.** **Never add oil** to the water.
- **Why:** less water gives starchier cooking water, which is a better emulsifier (P4). Sticking happens in the first 1–2 min, when the surface starch gelatinises, so stirring fixes it. Oil just floats on top.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats, "For the Best Pasta in Less Time…" https://www.seriouseats.com/the-best-way-to-boil-pasta-11986269 · Serious Eats, "The Right Way to Sauce Pasta" https://www.seriouseats.com/the-right-way-to-sauce-pasta · Serious Eats, "Cacio e Pepe" (pasta in a skillet, just covered) https://www.seriouseats.com/spaghetti-cacio-e-pepe-recipe

## P3. Al dente timing and finishing in the sauce
- **Rule:** drain dried pasta **about 1 min before the packet time** (for carbonara, "a bit firmer than al dente"). Finish it **in the sauce** for the last minute or two, loosening with pasta water. **Always reserve about 240 ml (1 cup) pasta water before draining.** Or lift the pasta out with tongs or a spider so the starchy water stays in the pot.
- **Fresh egg pasta:** **60–120 s** for thin noodles (Gritzer prefers about 90 s, and never more than 2 min, or it turns mushy). Thicker cuts take up to 3 min (NYT).
- **Why:** pasta keeps absorbing liquid in the sauce. Finishing it there lets starch thicken the sauce and bind it to the pasta.
- **Cue:** the pasta bends but has a thin, firm core when bitten. It is not chalky white in the middle.
- **Failure → fix:** if the pasta is soft or bloated, it was cooked to packet time and then simmered in sauce, so pull it 1–2 min early. If the dish is dry when served, there wasn't enough reserved water, so always keep 1 cup.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats, "The Right Way to Sauce Pasta" https://www.seriouseats.com/the-right-way-to-sauce-pasta · Serious Eats, "Cacio e Pepe" (about 1 min less than the packet) https://www.seriouseats.com/spaghetti-cacio-e-pepe-recipe · NYT Cooking, "Spaghetti Carbonara" (reserve 1 cup) https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/12965-spaghetti-carbonara · Serious Eats, "Homemade Fresh Pasta" (60–90 s, never more than 2 min) https://www.seriouseats.com/fresh-egg-pasta · NYT Cooking / Melissa Clark, "Fresh Egg Pasta" (1–3 min) https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/1017391-fresh-egg-pasta

## P4. The emulsion: pasta water + fat, tossing (mantecatura)
- **Rule (sequence):**
  1. Put the pasta, hot sauce and **about 2 tbsp pasta water per serving** in the pan.
  2. Add **fat if the sauce is lean** (a glug of extra-virgin olive oil or a knob of butter).
  3. **Simmer and toss hard** until it is glossy and coats the pasta.
  4. **Off the heat**, beat in the cheese and herbs, tossing hard (the Italian *mantecatura*).
  5. **Adjust with more pasta water right before serving.**
- **Why:** dissolved starch acts as an emulsifier and stabiliser. It binds fat and water into a creamy sauce that clings. Vigorous motion and a simmer break the fat into fine droplets. Cheese added over high heat with thin sauces seizes and clumps.
- **Cue:** the sauce is glossy and creamy. Each strand is coated, with no pool of oil or water on the plate. It slides slowly when you tilt the plate.
- **Failure → fix:** an oily, separated sauce needs more pasta water and vigorous tossing. A sauce that turns gluey or tight as it cools needs loosening with hot pasta water at the last moment. If the cheese is clumped and stringy, the pan was too hot, so add cheese off the heat (P5).
- **Sources:** Serious Eats, "The Right Way to Sauce Pasta" https://www.seriouseats.com/the-right-way-to-sauce-pasta · Serious Eats, "For the Best Pasta in Less Time…" (starch as emulsifier) https://www.seriouseats.com/the-best-way-to-boil-pasta-11986269 · Serious Eats, "Roman-Style Fettuccine Alfredo" (toss until an emulsified sauce forms) https://www.seriouseats.com/fettuccine-alfredo-sauce-italian-pasta-recipe

## P5. Cacio e pepe (cheese + pepper + pasta water)
- **Rule:**
  - **Cheese:** **very finely grated** Pecorino Romano on a Microplane, about **55–140 g per 225 g pasta** (depending on the recipe).
  - **Pepper:** toast coarse black pepper **about 1 min** in a dry pan or in oil until fragrant.
  - **Never combine the cheese with boiling-hot liquid.** Let the pasta **cool about 1 min** first. Add water in **¼-cup (60 ml)** additions.
  - **Foolproof method (Gritzer):** cook a **cornstarch gel** (1 tsp / 4 g cornstarch in 45 ml water, heated until clear), beat it into the cheese to make a paste, then loosen with pasta water.
  - **Classic method (Kenji):** pepper in olive oil, a little pasta water and butter, then the pasta, then the cheese stirred in until melted, adjusting with water.
- **Why:** Pecorino is aged, dry and low in moisture, so its proteins seize into stringy clumps when they overheat. Starch (from the water, or added cornstarch) coats the proteins and stabilises the sauce. Butter and oil give extra fat for the emulsion.
- **Failure → fix:** if the sauce is stringy or clumped, it got too hot or the cheese was too coarse. Add a gel or more starch, cool the pasta slightly first, and grate finer. You can't rescue it once it has clumped.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats / Gritzer, "Perfect Cacio e Pepe" https://www.seriouseats.com/perfect-cacio-e-pepe-sauce-pasta-recipe-8780373 · Serious Eats / Kenji, "Cacio e Pepe" https://www.seriouseats.com/spaghetti-cacio-e-pepe-recipe · Serious Eats, "The Right Way to Sauce Pasta" (cheese off heat in thin sauces) https://www.seriouseats.com/the-right-way-to-sauce-pasta

## P6. Carbonara
- **Rule:**
  - **Base:** render diced **guanciale** (or pancetta or bacon) in a little olive oil over medium heat until the fat runs and the edges crisp, **about 7 min**.
  - **Sauce:** per 450 g pasta, about **2 whole eggs + 6 yolks** (Serious Eats; NYT uses 2 + 2 per 340 g) with **about 50 g mixed Pecorino + Parmigiano** and plenty of black pepper. The eggs should be **at room temperature**.
  - **Combine off the direct heat:** move the pasta and the pork fat into the egg bowl, add about **120 ml pasta water**, then set the bowl over the pot of boiling pasta water and stir constantly **until it thickens and leaves trails** (it can still scramble, so keep stirring and move it to a warm bowl the moment it thickens). Or toss in a warmed bowl or pan off the heat.
  - Serve at once.
- **Why:** yolks thicken into a custard at about the temperature where they start to scramble. The gentle, indirect heat of the double boiler gives a wider window. More yolk gives a richer sauce that thickens more reliably. Cold eggs on hot pasta set unevenly into curds.
- **Cue:** a creamy sauce that coats the spoon and leaves clear trails, with no visible egg curds.
- **Failure → fix:** scrambled or curdled egg means it got too hot, so use the double boiler and keep stirring. If the sauce is thin and runny, it was underheated or had too much water, so keep stirring over the gentle heat.
- **Safety (site):** carbonara egg sauce does **not** reach FSIS's 71 °C for egg dishes. **The recipe must say to use pasteurised eggs** for anyone pregnant, elderly, very young or immunocompromised.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats / Kenji, "Spaghetti With Carbonara Sauce" https://www.seriouseats.com/pasta-carbonara-sauce-recipe · NYT Cooking, "Spaghetti Carbonara" (room-temperature eggs; off the heat; reserve water) https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/12965-spaghetti-carbonara · FSIS chart (egg dishes 160 °F / 71 °C) https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/safe-temperature-chart · FoodSafety.gov chart https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/safe-minimum-internal-temperatures

## P7. Alfredo (Roman butter-and-Parmigiano style)
- **Rule:** per 450 g **fresh fettuccine**: **115 g butter (diced) + 115 g finely grated Parmigiano-Reggiano** (younger than 18 months melts better, or Grana Padano) in a warm bowl. Add the drained pasta + **120 ml hot pasta water**, then **toss repeatedly** until the butter melts and the sauce turns creamy. Add splashes of water as needed. **No cream** in the Roman version.
- **Why:** the starchy water and the milk solids in the butter emulsify the melted fat with the cheese. Finely grated, younger cheese melts smoothly.
- **Failure → fix:** if the sauce is greasy or clumpy, there wasn't enough water or tossing, or the cheese was coarse or very aged.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats, "Roman-Style Fettuccine Alfredo" https://www.seriouseats.com/fettuccine-alfredo-sauce-italian-pasta-recipe · Serious Eats, "The Right Way to Sauce Pasta" https://www.seriouseats.com/the-right-way-to-sauce-pasta

## P8. Ragù alla bolognese
- **Rule (sequence, Hazan / ATK / Serious Eats agree on the core):**
  1. **Soffritto:** onion, then carrot and celery, in butter (and oil) over medium heat, until **translucent and soft, not browned** (about 6 min).
  2. **Meat:** add the beef, or beef + pork (+ veal), crumbled fine. Hazan and ATK cook it only **until it loses its raw colour**. Serious Eats **browns half the meat well** (about 15 min) for depth, then adds the rest.
  3. **Milk (Hazan/ATK):** about 240 ml per 340 g meat, simmered **until it has fully evaporated** (10–15 min). Add a tiny grating of nutmeg.
  4. **Wine:** dry white, about 240 ml, simmered until evaporated.
  5. **Tomatoes:** add them, then **the laziest simmer, uncovered, for 3 h or more**. Add 120 ml water when it dries out. At the end no water should remain and the fat separates. Stir the fat back in and correct the salt.
- **Why:** milk tenderises the meat and softens the acidity of the wine and tomato. A long, gentle reduction concentrates flavour and gives a thick, meaty sauce (not a soup). Browning part of the meat adds Maillard depth without making all of it hard.
- **Serving:** toss with the pasta and **a tablespoon of butter**, with Parmigiano on the side. A bolognese with very little sauce coats the pasta and doesn't pool.
- **Cue:** a thick, glossy sauce with the fat separating at the edges and no watery liquid. The meat is very tender.
- **Disagreement:** whether to brown the meat or just cook it until it loses its colour. **Pick:** either works. A recipe must state which, and never let the soffritto scorch.
- **Sources:** NYT Cooking, "Marcella Hazan's Bolognese Sauce" (adapted from *Essentials of Classic Italian Cooking*) https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/1015181-marcella-hazans-bolognese-sauce · ATK, "Classic Bolognese Sauce" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/recipes/536-classic-bolognese-sauce · Serious Eats, "Basic Ragù Bolognese" https://www.seriouseats.com/basic-ragu-bolognese-recipe

## P9. Simple tomato sauce (Hazan butter sauce)
- **Rule:** **2 cups (about 480 g) canned whole tomatoes with their juice**, plus **70 g (5 tbsp) butter** and **1 onion, peeled and halved**, plus salt. Simmer **uncovered for about 45 min** at medium (bring it to a simmer, then keep it gentle), stirring occasionally and mashing large pieces. Taste for salt. **Remove the onion** before tossing. This sauces 450 g of pasta.
- **Pass-2 correction:** NYT lists "2 cups tomatoes... (for example, a 28-ounce can)". An 800 g can is about 3 cups, so a full can gives a sharper, less buttery sauce. **Recipes should use 2 cups (about 480 g) for this butter level, or scale the butter up for a full can.** This is our reading, because the page shows both quantities.
- **Why:** butter fat rounds out the tomato's acidity and gives a velvety texture. The whole onion gives sweetness without onion pieces. Gritzer found raw aromatics give a cleaner, fresher-tasting but less integrated sauce than sautéed ones, and he names Hazan's whole-onion trick as the way to avoid the texture problem.
- **Sources:** NYT Cooking, "Marcella Hazan's Tomato Sauce" (re-read in full in pass 2) https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/1015178-marcella-hazans-tomato-sauce · Serious Eats / Daniel Gritzer, "The Best Fresh Tomato Sauce" (names Hazan's raw-onion-halves method; finish with butter, olive oil or cream) https://www.seriouseats.com/the-best-fresh-tomato-sauce-for-pasta-recipe · Serious Eats, "The Right Way to Sauce Pasta" (add fat to lean sauces) https://www.seriouseats.com/the-right-way-to-sauce-pasta
- **Status:** the full recipe numbers (butter, time, onion) come **only from the NYT adaptation**, which is the only readable Hazan text on an allowed outlet. The method is confirmed by Gritzer. The PBS and Food52 reprints that quote her book ("until the fat floats free") are **not on the locked list, so they are not cited**.

## P10. Fresh egg pasta dough
- **Rule (ratio):** **egg (whole + extra yolks) ≈ 60–70 % of flour weight**, with a little salt.
  - Serious Eats: 255 g AP flour + 2 eggs + 4 yolks (180 g egg, 71 %) + 1 tsp kosher salt.
  - NYT / Melissa Clark: 290 g flour + 2 eggs + 3 yolks + 1 tbsp olive oil (about 55–60 % egg + oil) + 3 g salt. Use 00 for silkier pasta, bread flour for more chew.
  - **Knead until smooth and elastic** like firm play-dough (**about 10 min** by hand). **Rest it wrapped for 30 min–1 h** at room temperature (overnight in the fridge is fine).
  - **Roll:** widest setting, fold and pass 2–3 times, then narrow one notch at a time. Stop **1–2 settings before the thinnest for fettuccine and pappardelle**. Use the thinnest for lasagne and filled pasta. The sheet should be slightly translucent.
- **Why:** whites hydrate and make the dough supple, and yolks add richness, colour and tenderness. The rest lets the flour hydrate and the gluten relax. Gritzer's test found no-rest dough firmer and more rubbery, and doughs rested 1 h or more were about the same.
- **Cue:** the dough is smooth and not sticky. It springs back slowly when pressed.
- **Failure → fix:** if the dough crumbles and won't come together, it is too dry, so mist it with water or add a little oil. If it sticks, it is too wet, so add flour 1 tsp at a time. If it shrinks back or tears when rolled, it wasn't rested.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats / Gritzer, "Homemade Fresh Pasta" https://www.seriouseats.com/fresh-egg-pasta · NYT Cooking / Melissa Clark, "Fresh Egg Pasta" https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/1017391-fresh-egg-pasta
