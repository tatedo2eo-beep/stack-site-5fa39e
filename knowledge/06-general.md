## G1. Salt by weight: the house numbers
- **Rule (final dish, salt as % of total food weight):**

| Food | Salt % by weight | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Lean meat/poultry (turkey breast, pork loin) | **0.5 %** | ATK taste test |
| Moderate fat (strip steak, 90/10 beef) | **0.75 %** | ATK |
| Fatty (80/20 beef, burgers, sausage-style mixes) | **1 %** | ATK; Serious Eats 1–2 % general range |
| Soups, sauces, stews, general savoury dishes | **about 1 %, tested within 0.8–1.2 %** | Serious Eats: people prefer 1–2 % |
| Pasta cooking water | **1 %** (0.5 % for salty sauces) | see 04-pasta P1 |
| Dry brine for chicken or meat | **0.5–0.7 %**, applied 1 h–overnight ahead (02-C2) | ATK, AmazingRibs |

- **Weigh the salt (grams) and name the type.** Diamond Crystal kosher salt is about half as dense by volume as table salt or Morton kosher. A teaspoon of one salt "can weigh twice as much as another".
- **Season in stages:** salt early (it draws out water from onions and mushrooms, which then brown faster, and it penetrates proteins). Taste and adjust at the end. **Under-salt anything that will reduce**, because water evaporates but the salt stays.
- **Raw mixes and batters can't be tasted**, so give an exact gram weight (meatballs, sausage, meatloaf, dough).
- **Why:** fat dulls taste, so fattier food needs more salt.
- **Fixes for over-salting:**
  - Dilute with unsalted liquid.
  - Add bulk (starch or vegetables).
  - Add cream or yogurt.
  - In dressings, add fat or acid, or a touch of sugar.
- **Sources:** ATK, "Does Fattier Meat Need More Salt?" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/6597-does-fattier-meat-need-more-salt · Serious Eats / Leah Colins, "What 'Season to Taste' Really Means" https://www.seriouseats.com/tips-for-seasoning-with-salt-11956480 · Serious Eats, "How Salty Should Pasta Water Be?" https://www.seriouseats.com/how-salty-should-pasta-water-be

## G2. Balance: acid, fat, sweetness, heat, texture
- **Rule (checks for any savoury recipe):**
  - **Acid:** a squeeze of lemon or a splash of vinegar at the end brightens a flat dish and balances bitterness or salt. House check: if a rich, fatty or starchy dish has no acid element (citrus, vinegar, wine, tomato, yoghurt, pickles), consider adding one.
  - **Acid in marinades denatures surface proteins.** Too much, or for too long, makes meat mushy, so cap acidic marinades (see 02-chicken C10).
  - **Fat** carries flavour and rounds out acidity (for example, the butter in Hazan's tomato sauce, 04-P9). Lean sauces need added fat to emulsify (04-P4).
  - **Sweetness** (honey, sugar) tames harshness from salt or acid.
  - **Texture contrast:** every dish should have one crisp, crunchy or fresh element against a soft one (crisp-edged smash burger with a soft bun; toasted crumbs on pasta; raw herbs on a braise). This is house practice, not a sourced number.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats, "6 Chemistry-Based Cooking Tricks" (acid as seasoning; marinades) https://www.seriouseats.com/game-changing-ph-tips-11812345 · Serious Eats, "What 'Season to Taste' Really Means" (balancing with fat, acid and sweetness) https://www.seriouseats.com/tips-for-seasoning-with-salt-11956480 · NYT Cooking, "Marcella Hazan's Tomato Sauce" https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/1015178-marcella-hazans-tomato-sauce

## G3. Flavour layering (sequence)
- **Rule:**
  1. Brown proteins first and remove them.
  2. Soften aromatics in the fat, salted.
  3. Fry tomato paste and ground spices in fat for ~10 s to 2 min (see 02-C9).
  4. Deglaze with wine or stock, scraping up the fond.
  5. Simmer, then finish with fresh elements (acid, herbs, butter or cheese) off the heat.
  - **Taste at each stage.**
- **Why:** each stage builds a different flavour: Maillard browning from the meat, sweetness from the aromatics, spices blooming in fat, concentration from the reduction, and lift from the fresh finish.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats, "Basic Ragù Bolognese" (browning, tomato paste 2 min, wine reduction) https://www.seriouseats.com/basic-ragu-bolognese-recipe · ATK, "Classic Bolognese Sauce" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/recipes/536-classic-bolognese-sauce · Serious Eats, "Season to Taste" (season in stages) https://www.seriouseats.com/tips-for-seasoning-with-salt-11956480 · 02-chicken C9 curry-base sources.

## G4. Browning (Maillard)
- **Rule:**
  - **A dry surface plus a hot pan.** Pat meat dry; salting well ahead dries the surface.
  - **Don't crowd the pan.**
  - For searing, heat the oil **until it smokes lightly** (visible wisps).
  - **Shallow-frying cutlets: shimmering, about 135 °C**.
  - A pinch of baking soda (alkaline) speeds browning (onions, potatoes, pretzels), but too much turns vegetables mushy (Serious Eats pH tips) and gives chicken a metallic bitterness (see 02-C4).
- **Why:** browning reactions need surface temperatures well above 100 °C. Surface water holds the food at boiling point until it evaporates. Oil below its smoke point browns too slowly, so the inside overcooks first.
- **Cue:** a deep golden-brown crust that releases from the pan easily.
- **Failure → fix:** grey, steamed meat means the pan was crowded, the surface was wet or the oil was too cool. Bitter, black fond means the heat was too high for too long, so lower the heat and deglaze earlier.
- **Sources:** ATK, "Shimmer, Deep-Fry, and Smoke" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/2977-shimmer-deep-fry-and-smoke-how-oil-behaves-at-different-temperatures · Serious Eats, "6 Chemistry-Based Cooking Tricks" (alkaline browning) https://www.seriouseats.com/game-changing-ph-tips-11812345 · Serious Eats, "Season to Taste" (early salt helps onions and mushrooms brown) https://www.seriouseats.com/tips-for-seasoning-with-salt-11956480 · see also 01-burgers B5, 02-chicken C2.
- **Note:** the "surface water holds food at boiling point" explanation is standard food science (it is in McGee, but not verified online here). The explanation is ours; the rules are sourced.

## G5. Heat control and oil temperatures
- **Rule (ATK oil stages):**

| Stage | Temp | Visual cue | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shimmering | ~135 °C (275 °F) | gleams, moves in ripples | sautéing, shallow-frying cutlets |
| Deep-fry start | 165–190 °C (325–375 °F) | a bread cube sizzles with fine bubbles | deep frying |
| Smoking | 175–270 °C (350–520 °F), depending on the oil | several wisps of smoke | searing steak, stir-fry |

- **Gentle heat** (a cold-pan start for chicken thighs to render the fat; a low oven at about 120 °C for reverse sear) prevents tough, overcooked protein. Resting is temperature control: rest until the thermometer reads the target, not by the clock.
- **Sources:** ATK, "Shimmer, Deep-Fry, and Smoke" (above) · Serious Eats, "Game-changing temperature tips" https://www.seriouseats.com/game-changing-temperature-tips-11803604 · Serious Eats, "Meat resting science" https://www.seriouseats.com/meat-resting-science-11776272

## G6. Deep- and shallow-frying safety
- **Rule:**
  - **Equipment:** a heavy, deep pot, **filled no more than half full** (house rule; neither source gives a fill level), with a **clip-on thermometer**. Use only metal or wood tools, never plastic.
  - **Oil:** neutral, with a high smoke point (peanut, canola, sunflower, vegetable, grapeseed). The **smoke point should be at least 28 °C (50 °F) above the frying temperature.**
  - **Temperature:** **165–175 °C (325–350 °F)** is the default. Fry in small batches so it doesn't drop too far. FSIS reference times: chicken pieces 13–20 min at 190 °C; chicken fingers 6–8 min at 175 °C; fish fillets 3–5 min at 160 °C; shrimp 4–6 min at 160 °C.
  - **Water:** **dry the food first.** Water hitting hot oil turns instantly to steam and spatters or boils over.
  - **Never leave hot oil unattended.**
  - **Grease fire:** **never use water.** Smother it with a metal lid or use a kitchen fire extinguisher, turn off the heat, and call emergency services.
  - **Doneness:** check the internal temperature **out of the oil** (poultry 74 °C). Return to the oil if it's under.
  - **After frying:** cool the oil about 2 h. Strain and reuse it if it's clean. Throw it out if it is cloudy, foaming or smells off. **Never pour it down the sink.** Refrigerate leftovers within 2 h.
- **Sources:** FSIS, "Deep Fat Frying and Food Safety" https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/deep-fat-frying-and-food-safety · Serious Eats, "How to Deep-Fry at Home" https://www.seriouseats.com/how-to-deep-fry-at-home-without-a-deep-fryer-tips · Serious Eats, "What's a Smoke Point" https://www.seriouseats.com/cooking-fats-101-whats-a-smoke-point-and-why-does-it-matter · ATK, "Shimmer, Deep-Fry, and Smoke" (above)
- **Disagreements:**
  - FSIS lists olive oil as fine for deep frying, while Serious Eats recommends a neutral refined fat. **Pick:** neutral refined oil for recipes.
  - FSIS advises against cast iron for frying (it breaks down oil faster), while Serious Eats allows any deep, heavy pot. **Pick:** "a heavy, deep pot or Dutch oven".

## G7. Emulsions and sauces
- **Vinaigrette:**
  - **2–3 parts oil to 1 part acid** by volume. 3 : 1 is the default, and you can swap some vinegar for water to make it less sharp.
  - **Emulsifier:** at least 1 tsp Dijon mustard per tbsp vinegar (Kenji). ATK uses ½ tsp mustard + ½ tsp mayo per 1 tbsp vinegar + 3 tbsp oil. Mayo emulsifies even better. Molasses or honey stabilises it for make-ahead dressings.
  - **Method:** whisk the vinegar and seasonings, then add the oil in a thin stream. Or shake in a jar: ATK shakes about 30 s, adding oil in stages. A blender gives the most stable result.
  - **Why emulsify:** unemulsified oil wilts greens, and the vinegar drains to the bottom of the bowl.
- **Pan sauces and pasta:** starchy pasta water, cheese or mustard emulsify fat into water. Finish with cold butter or cheese **off the heat** (see 04-P4).
- **Egg emulsions and custards:** use gentle, indirect heat and constant stirring (carbonara, 04-P6). Egg-thickened sauces scramble if they get too hot.
- **Failure → fix:** if a vinaigrette separates, add more mustard or mayo and whisk again. If a cheese sauce clumps, it got too hot, so lower the heat and add starch (see 04-P5; the Serious Eats melting-cheese article).
- **Sources:** Serious Eats / Kenji, "What's the Point of a Vinaigrette?" https://www.seriouseats.com/salad-dressings-vinaigrettes-the-food-lab · ATK, "How to Make Great Vinaigrettes" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/7656-how-to-make-great-vinaigrettes · ATK, "Basic Vinaigrette" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/recipes/7137-basic-vinaigrette · Serious Eats, "The Science of Melting Cheese" https://www.seriouseats.com/the-science-of-melting-cheese

## G9. Food-safety temperatures (site minimums)
| Food | Site minimum (core) | FSIS / FoodSafety.gov |
|---|---|---|
| Ground beef, pork, lamb, veal (burgers, meatballs, meatloaf) | **71 °C** | 160 °F |
| All poultry (whole, pieces, ground), stuffing | **74 °C**, measured before it leaves the heat | 165 °F |
| Whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, veal (steaks, chops, roasts) | **63 °C + 3 min rest** | 145 °F + 3 min |
| Fish | **63 °C**, or flesh no longer translucent and flaking | 145 °F |
| Shrimp, lobster, crab, scallops | flesh pearly or white and opaque | (cue only) |
| Clams, mussels, oysters | shells open (discard any that stay shut; house rule) | (cue only) |
| Rabbit, venison | **71 °C** | 160 °F |
| Egg dishes (quiche, frittata, casseroles) | **71 °C** | 160 °F |
| Leftovers, casseroles, reheating | **74 °C** | 165 °F |
- **Holding and storage:**
  - **Danger zone 4–60 °C (40–140 °F)**, where bacteria can double in about 20 min.
  - **Never leave food out for more than 2 h, or 1 h above 32 °C (90 °F).**
  - Keep hot food at 60 °C or above.
  - Cool leftovers in **shallow containers** and refrigerate within 2 h. The **fridge should be at 4 °C or below** and the freezer at −18 °C.
  - **Cooked leftovers keep 3–4 days.** Raw ground meat and raw poultry keep 1–2 days, and raw steaks, chops and roasts 3–5 days.
  - **Thaw and marinate in the fridge, never on the counter.** Don't rinse raw meat, poultry or eggs.
  - Use a separate board for raw meat.
- **Recipe implications:**
  - Medium-rare burgers can never appear on the site.
  - Carbonara, mayonnaise, tiramisu and mousse need a pasteurised-egg note.
  - Raw cookie dough or flour needs a do-not-eat-raw note (03-K10).
- **Sources:** FSIS, Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/safe-temperature-chart · FoodSafety.gov, Safe Minimum Internal Temperatures https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/safe-minimum-internal-temperatures · FSIS, "Danger Zone (40 °F–140 °F)" https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/danger-zone-40f-140f · FoodSafety.gov, "4 Steps to Food Safety" https://www.foodsafety.gov/keep-food-safe/4-steps-to-food-safety · FoodSafety.gov, Cold Food Storage Chart https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/cold-food-storage-charts

## G10. Stocks
- **Rule (white chicken stock, Serious Eats):**
  - Use **1.8–3.6 kg chicken parts per 3.8 L water**. Include **wings or feet for gelatin**, plus some meat (not just bones) for flavour.
  - Add diced onion, carrot, celery, garlic and parsley. Raw (not sautéed) aromatics tasted cleanest in the test.
  - Start cold, bring up over low heat, and hold at a **very gentle simmer (never a boil) for about 1½ h**.
  - Fine-strain, chill, then lift off the fat.
  - **Thin stock fix:** 1 packet unflavoured gelatin, softened in ¼ cup cold water, per 1 L stock.
- **Brown/quick variant (ATK):**
  - Brown 1.8 kg chicken pieces in batches.
  - Cover and sweat on low about 20 min until the juices release.
  - Add 2 L **boiling** water and barely simmer about 20 min. Strain and defat.
- **Pressure cooker (ATK):**
  - 1½ h gives a **darker, meatier** stock; a pot gives a **cleaner chicken** taste.
  - Use pressure stock for braises and stews, pot stock for clear soups.
- **Why:**
  - Collagen from skin, joints and wings converts to gelatin, which gives body.
  - A gentle simmer keeps fat and proteins from emulsifying into cloudiness. Serious Eats: a very gentle simmer removes the need to skim.
  - Pressure-cooker heat extracts more flavour compounds and breaks proteins into savoury peptides.
- **Cue:** a chilled stock that sets to a soft jelly has good body. If it is still thin, use the gelatin fix.
- **Failure → fix:**
  - Cloudy or greasy → it boiled. Keep it below a simmer, fine-strain, and chill to defat.
  - Thin → add wings or feet, or the gelatin fix.
  - Flat → add more meat, or use the pressure method.
- **Storage (disagreement → safer pick):** ATK says up to **2 days** chilled; Serious Eats says 5 days. **House: chill quickly and use within 2 days, or freeze.** Frozen stock keeps for months: "several" per ATK, up to 6 per SE. Cool and reheat per G9 (reheat leftovers to 74 °C).
- **Sources:**
  - Serious Eats, "Rich and Easy White Chicken Stock": https://www.seriouseats.com/best-rich-easy-white-chicken-stock-recipe
  - ATK, "Making Stock: Pot versus Pressure Cooker": https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/6585-making-stock-pot-versus-pressure-cooker
  - ATK, "Chicken Stock with Ginger and Scallions": https://www.americastestkitchen.com/recipes/5140-asian-chicken-stock

## G11. Mother sauces (Escoffier's five) and roux
- **The five (Le Cordon Bleu):**
  - **Béchamel:** milk + white roux. Mornay = béchamel + cheese; soubise = béchamel + onion.
  - **Velouté:** light stock (chicken or fish) + white or blond roux. Suprême = velouté + cream; allemande = velouté + yolks, lemon and cream.
  - **Espagnole:** brown stock + brown roux + mirepoix + tomato purée. It is the base of demi-glace.
  - **Sauce tomate:** reduced tomatoes. Roux is traditional but optional; for Italian style see 04-P9.
  - **Hollandaise:** warm melted butter emulsified into egg yolks with acid. Béarnaise adds a reduction of vinegar, shallot and tarragon.
- **Roux rule:**
  - Roughly **equal parts fat and flour**. Cook until the raw-flour smell is gone, about 1 min for white roux.
  - Add the liquid **gradually while whisking**: warm milk for béchamel. The sauce goes very thick, then thin, then thickens again at the simmer.
  - Thickness per 240 ml liquid (Serious Eats):

    | Consistency | Flour per 240 ml | Fat |
    |---|---|---|
    | Light, gravy-like | 1–2 tbsp | equal amount |
    | Standard béchamel | 1½ tbsp (12 g) | 1½ tbsp butter (20 g) |
    | Thick (soufflé or moussaka base) | 3 tbsp | equal amount |

  - Simmer about 3 min, until it coats a spoon.
- **Why:** gelatinised flour starch thickens the liquid; cooking the roux first removes the raw taste. **The darker the roux, the less it thickens**, because browning breaks starch chains. A brown roux needs more flour for the same body.
- **Cue:** the sauce coats the back of a spoon and a finger drawn through it leaves a clear line. Press plastic onto the surface to stop a skin forming.
- **Failure → fix:**
  - Lumps → whisk hard or use a hand blender.
  - Raw or pasty taste → cook the roux longer, then simmer the sauce at least 3 min.
  - Too thin with a dark roux → expected; use more roux.
  - Velouté and espagnole: simmer and skim for clarity (LCB).
- **Hollandaise (blender method, Serious Eats):**
  1. Put 1 yolk, 1 tsp water, 1 tsp lemon juice and salt in a **narrow cup that just fits the immersion blender**.
  2. Heat **113 g butter until the foaming subsides**, then stream it in with the blender running.
  3. Thin with warm water, 1 tbsp at a time.
  - Hold in a warm place for **up to 1 h**. It **cannot be cooled and reheated**.
  - Classic method (LCB): whisk the yolks over a bain-marie and add the butter slowly. **If it splits, whisk in a spoon of warm water or a fresh yolk.**
  - **Safety (house):** the yolks are only lightly cooked, so recipe copy must carry the **pasteurised-egg note** (site override).
- **Sources:**
  - Le Cordon Bleu, "What are the mother sauces of French cooking?": https://www.cordonbleu.edu/news/what-are-the-mother-sauces-of-french-cooking/en
  - Le Cordon Bleu Malaysia, "How to Make the Perfect Sauce": https://www.cordonbleu.edu/malaysia/blog-how-to-make-the-perfect-sauce-mastering-barnaise-bchamel-more/en
  - Serious Eats, "Classic Béchamel": https://www.seriouseats.com/bechamel-white-sauce-recipe
  - Serious Eats, "How to Make a Roux": https://www.seriouseats.com/a-brief-guide-to-roux
  - Serious Eats, "Quick Immersion Blender Hollandaise": https://www.seriouseats.com/foolproof-2-minute-hollandaise-recipe

## G12. Rice and grains
- **Key rule:** in absorption cooking, **rice absorbs about its own volume of water, about 1 : 1**. Anything extra is only there to **evaporate**, and evaporation depends on the pot, lid and heat, **not on the batch size**. **Never scale the water linearly** (ATK): ATK's 1½ cups rice : 2¼ cups water pilaf **doubles to 3 cups rice : 3¾ cups water, not 4½**.
- **Working ratios by volume, rinsed rice, covered pot:**

  | Rice | Rice : water | Simmer | Rest | Source |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Long-grain or basmati pilaf | 1 : 1.5 | covered on low 16–18 min | 10 min under a towel | ATK |
  | Thai jasmine | 1 : 1.25 (185 g : 300 ml) | about 20 min | 5–10 min | SE |
  | Sealed-pot method | 1 : 1 | 15–25 min | 5 min | SE (pot wrapped and weighted so almost nothing evaporates) |

- **Method (pilaf, ATK):**
  1. **Rinse** 4–5 times until the water runs almost clear.
  2. Sweat the onion in butter.
  3. **Toast the rice about 3 min, until the edges turn translucent.**
  4. Add **hot** salted water (1½ tsp table salt per 1½ cups rice), boil, cover, and cook on low.
  5. Off the heat, put a **folded towel under the lid for 10 min**, then fluff.
- **Pasta method (Serious Eats test):**
  - Boil the rice in plenty of water (1 cup basmati in 2 qt, about 10 min) and drain.
  - Grains come out **plumper and more separate**, with a slightly lighter flavour.
  - The margin for error is small. For biryani or tahdig, **drain at 80–90 % done** and finish by steaming.
- **Risotto (Serious Eats classic):**
  1. Use 400 g carnaroli to up to 2 L hot stock. Sweat the onion and **toast the rice 2–3 min, until translucent at the edges**.
  2. Add the wine, then add stock **½ cup at a time, stirring**. It takes 15–20 min, until the rice is al dente.
  3. **Off the heat, beat in cold butter and Parmigiano.**
  4. Serve loose, "like lava", on warm plates.
  - **Single source, marked:** the risotto numbers rest on Serious Eats only.
- **Why:**
  - Rinsing removes surface starch that would glue the grains.
  - Toasting coats the grains in fat and starts the flavour.
  - The towel absorbs condensation so water doesn't drip back and make the top layer soggy.
  - In absorption cooking the last liquid is starchy, which makes the grains cling. Draining removes that starch.
- **Cue:** tilt the pot or push a spatula to the bottom. **No water left** means it is done. If the bottom is dry but the rice is still firm, rest it covered 5–10 min, or sprinkle on 2 tbsp water and cook on low (SE jasmine).
- **Failure → fix:**
  - Mushy, especially on doubled batches → too much water for the evaporation; use the ATK rule.
  - Gummy → not rinsed, or stirred during the simmer.
  - Crunchy → under-watered or the lid leaked; add 2 tbsp water, cover and steam.
  - Scorched → heat too high; use the lowest setting.
- **Safety:** cool leftover rice quickly and keep it chilled. The Serious Eats jasmine page allows 1 week, which is **longer than the house leftovers rule (G9)**, so **follow G9**. Reheat to 74 °C.
- **Sources:**
  - ATK, "Basic Rice Pilaf": https://www.americastestkitchen.com/recipes/516-simple-rice-pilaf
  - ATK, "Science: The Secrets of Cooking Rice": https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/9395-science-the-secrets-of-cooking-rice
  - Serious Eats, "Basic White Rice": https://www.seriouseats.com/basic-white-rice-recipe-5189757
  - Serious Eats, "Perfect Stovetop Thai Jasmine Rice": https://www.seriouseats.com/perfect-stovetop-thai-jasmine-rice
