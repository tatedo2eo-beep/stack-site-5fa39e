# 02 · Chicken

> **Private reference. Not for publication.** Chef and outlet names stay in this file only. All sources were opened on 22 Oct 2026 and are on `SOURCES-LOCKED.md`. **[V]** means the text comes from the written companion page of an official chef video. I did not watch the video.
> Salt conversions (ATK): 3 tsp Diamond Crystal kosher = 2¼ tsp Morton kosher = 1½ tsp table salt. Write recipes in grams.

**Site safety override:** all chicken in our recipes reaches **74 °C / 165 °F** in the thickest part **as measured before it comes off the heat** (FSIS, FoodSafety.gov). Serious Eats and some chefs pull breasts at 65 °C (150 °F) and rely on time at temperature. That is scientifically valid but needs careful probing, so **we don't use it** (see C1).

---

## C1. Safe temperature, probing and carryover
- **Rule:** every cut (breast, thigh, drumstick, wing, whole bird, mince) cooks to **74 °C**. Probe the thickest part of the breast, and the thigh near but not touching the bone. FSIS lists **no rest time** for poultry, so the 74 °C reading must happen before the chicken leaves the heat. Thighs, legs and wings take higher temperatures well and are better above 74 °C. Breast dries out fast above 74 °C, so pull it as soon as it reads 74 °C.
- **Why:** at 74 °C, Salmonella is reduced 7-log almost instantly. Lower temperatures need a hold time (FSIS-based table via Serious Eats: 65 °C for 4.2 min at 12 % fat; 63 °C for 13 min). That is only safe if the coldest point is measured correctly, which home cooks can't guarantee.
- **Carryover:** resting does **not** make meat "reabsorb juices". Rest is for temperature only. Carryover can add 3–8 °C (5–15 °F) in minutes on thick cuts. Use it only to rest for serving (10–20 min for a whole bird), **never to reach the safe temperature**.
- **Failure → fix:** if the breast is dry and the thighs are fine, the breast overshot, so start checking it earlier and pull pieces as each one reaches 74 °C (Serious Eats removes fried pieces individually as they reach temperature).
- **Sources:** USDA FSIS, Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart https://www.fsis.usda.gov/food-safety/safe-food-handling-and-preparation/food-safety-basics/safe-temperature-chart · FoodSafety.gov chart https://www.foodsafety.gov/food-safety-charts/safe-minimum-internal-temperatures · Serious Eats, "Stop Overcooking Chicken" (time-temperature table; 165 °F is the simplest, most foolproof rule; dark meat better hotter) https://www.seriouseats.com/safe-chicken-temperature-time-and-temp-11948586 · Serious Eats / Kenji, "Sous Vide Chicken Breast" guide https://www.seriouseats.com/the-food-lab-complete-guide-to-sous-vide-chicken-breast · Serious Eats, "This Major Rule About Cooking Meat Turns Out to Be Wrong" (resting = carryover control; 5–15 °F carryover) https://www.seriouseats.com/meat-resting-science-11776272

## C2. Dry brine (pre-salting): the default
- **Rule:** salt chicken ahead, uncovered on a rack in the fridge:
  - **Amount:** **about 0.5–0.7 % of the meat weight** in salt. ATK's 1 tsp Diamond kosher per 450 g is about 3 g, or 0.65 %. Meathead's ½ tsp kosher per lb (Morton shown in his photos; ¼ tsp table salt) is about 2.4 g, or 0.5 %. Lean breast tastes seasoned at about 0.5–0.75 % (ATK's taste test), so use **0.5–0.7 %**, weighed. *(Pass 2 correction: this line previously said "about 1 %", which the cited figures don't support.)*
  - **Time:** pieces and cutlets need **at least 45 min–4 h**. A **whole bird needs 6–24 h** (ATK), and Serious Eats allows up to 3 days. Do not rinse it off.
  - For skin-on pieces, salt under the skin as well as on it.
- **Why:** salt first draws out liquid, then dissolves into it and diffuses back in. It loosens proteins so they hold more water during cooking. The surface ends up drier, so it browns and crisps better. A wet brine adds water, which dilutes flavour and softens the skin.
- **Cue:** after a few hours the skin looks dry and taut, with no beads of liquid.
- **Disagreement:** dry-brine times range from 2–4 h minimum (Meathead) to at least 12 h (Serious Eats, whole birds). **Pick:** whole birds overnight (12–24 h); pieces at least 1 h.
- **Failure → fix:** if the skin is soggy or pale, the bird was covered or wet brined, so dry brine it uncovered on a rack. If it tastes too salty, the salt was measured by volume with Morton or table salt, so weigh it.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats, "Dry Brine Is the Best Way to Brine" https://www.seriouseats.com/how-to-dry-brine · ATK, "How to Salt Chicken and How to Brine Chicken" (1 tsp kosher per lb, 6–24 h, whole bird) https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/610-how-to-salt-chicken-and-how-to-brine-chicken · Meathead / AmazingRibs, "Dry Brining" (½ tsp kosher per lb, or ¼ tsp table salt; poultry at least 2–4 h, overnight fine; rack, uncovered; re-read in pass 2) https://amazingribs.com/tested-recipes/salting-brining-curing-and-injecting/dry-brining/ · ATK, "Does Fattier Meat Need More Salt?" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/6597-does-fattier-meat-need-more-salt

## C3. Wet brine: only when it earns its place
- **Rule:** use a **6–9 % salt solution by weight** (60–90 g salt per 1 kg water), cold and refrigerated, for a **short time**. Boneless breasts and pieces need **30 min–1 h**, and a whole chicken (1.6–1.8 kg) needs **30 min–1 h** in ATK's strong brine. Never go longer than the recipe says. Pat it very dry afterwards. Use a wet brine for **skinless lean breast, cutlets and fried chicken**, not for crisp roast skin.
  - ATK: 2 qt (1.9 L) water + ½ cup (≈ 146 g) table salt for 1.8 kg pieces, ½–1 h (≈ 7.7 %). Fried chicken also gets ½ cup sugar.
  - Fried chicken in buttermilk: the buttermilk is the brine. Serious Eats uses 1 tbsp kosher salt + spices in buttermilk for **4 h to overnight**.
- **Why:** salt water dissolves some muscle proteins, so the meat holds more water. In AmazingRibs' test (Greg Blonder; 6 % brine for 8 h on a boneless breast, cooked to 160 °F), the wet-brined breast gained 10 % in raw weight and lost only 8 % of its raw weight in cooking, against 15 % unbrined and 12 % dry-brined, but tasted slightly less "chickeny". AmazingRibs recommends wet brine for lean, quick-cooking meats (breast, pork loin, fish) and dry brine for skin-on poultry, because wet brine makes skin soggy. ATK found 6 % absorbs slightly more water, while tasters preferred 9 % for seasoning.
- **Failure → fix:** if the meat is spongy, ham-like or salty, it was brined too long or too strong, so cut the time. If the crust or skin won't brown, it wasn't dried well enough.
- **Sources:** ATK, "A Better Brine?" (6 % vs 9 %) https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/6428-a-better-brine · ATK, "Brining Meat" (chart of times and amounts) https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/5804-brining-meat · ATK, "How to Salt Chicken and How to Brine Chicken" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/610-how-to-salt-chicken-and-how-to-brine-chicken · AmazingRibs (Meathead's site; article by Dave Joachim on Greg Blonder's experiments), "Wet Brining vs. Dry Brining" (full page read via WebFetch in pass 2) https://amazingribs.com/bbq-technique-and-science/wet-brining-vs-dry-brining/ · Serious Eats, "Buttermilk-Brined Southern Fried Chicken" https://www.seriouseats.com/the-food-lab-southern-fried-chicken-recipe

## C4. Crisp skin: dry surface + baking powder
- **Rule:** pat dry, then toss wings or rub skin with **baking powder ≈ 1 tsp (5 g) per 450 g** plus the salt. Rest uncovered on a rack in the fridge **8–24 h** (Serious Eats) for the crispest result, or roast straight away (ATK). Use **baking powder, never baking soda** (Serious Eats' soda-treated wings had a metallic bitterness).
- **Why:** the alkaline powder raises the skin's pH, so proteins break down and brown faster. It also forms tiny CO₂ bubbles that add surface area for crunch. Drying removes water, which would otherwise have to steam off first.
- **Cue:** the skin is deep golden, blistered and taut, and sounds crisp when tapped.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats, "The Best Oven-Fried Chicken Wings" https://www.seriouseats.com/the-best-buffalo-wings-oven-fried-wings-recipe · ATK, "Oven-Fried Chicken Wings" (1 tbsp baking powder per 1.8 kg) https://www.americastestkitchen.com/recipes/7583-oven-fried-chicken-wings · ATK, "Baking Powder: The Secret Weapon for Crispy Chicken Skin" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/3717-baking-powder-the-secret-weapon-for-crispy-chicken-skin · Serious Eats, "Dry Brine" (baking powder raises pH; bubbles) https://www.seriouseats.com/how-to-dry-brine

## C5. Wings
- **Oven method (rule):** bake the wings (with baking powder and salt, C4) on a **rack** over a foil-lined tray.
  - **Serious Eats:** **230 °C / 450 °F** on the upper-middle rack, 20 min, flip, then **15–30 min more**.
  - **ATK:** **120 °C / 250 °F for 30 min** on the lower-middle rack to render the fat, then **220 °C / 425 °F for 40–50 min** on the upper-middle rack, then rest 5 min.
  - Toss in the sauce **after** baking, in a bowl. Never bake wings in a wet sauce.
- **Fried method, double (confit) fry (rule):**
  1. Cook the wings gently in oil at **107–120 °C (225–250 °F) for about 20 min**, until tender but not browned.
  2. Rest **at least 1 h**, or up to 3 days chilled.
  3. Fry at **190–205 °C (375–400 °F) for about 10 min**, in batches of about a third.
- **Why:** low heat renders the fat under the skin and dries it. The final high heat then blisters it fast without overcooking the meat.
- **Sauce:** classic buffalo is **butter : hot sauce about 1 : 2 by volume**. Serious Eats uses 1 stick (113 g) butter + 1 cup (240 ml) Frank's per 1.8 kg wings, melted gently together over medium-low heat. Toss the wings just before serving.
- **Cue:** the skin is deep golden, crackling and puffed. The meat pulls from the bone and reads ≥ 74 °C.
- **Failure → fix:** if the skin is flabby, the wings were not dried, crowded with no rack, or sauced before crisping. If the sauce splits (greasy), heat it gently and whisk, without boiling.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats, "The Best Oven-Fried Chicken Wings" https://www.seriouseats.com/the-best-buffalo-wings-oven-fried-wings-recipe · ATK, "Oven-Fried Chicken Wings" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/recipes/7583-oven-fried-chicken-wings · Serious Eats, "Ultimate Extra-Crispy Double-Fried Confit Buffalo Wings" https://www.seriouseats.com/ultimate-extra-crispy-double-fried-confit-buffalo-wings

## C6. Deep-frying temperature and the double fry (fried chicken)
- **Rule (oil):** use a heavy pot filled **no more than half full** of neutral oil. **Preheat to 175–190 °C (350–375 °F).** The oil drops when the chicken goes in, so **hold it at 150–165 °C (300–325 °F)** while bone-in pieces fry. Fry in **batches** (about half a chicken at a time). Use a clip-on thermometer.
- **Bone-in pieces:** Serious Eats preheats very hot (about 220 °C / 425 °F), which drops to about 150 °C when the chicken goes in. Fry about 6 min a side until deep golden, then **finish on a rack in a 175 °C / 350 °F oven** until the breast reads (for us) 74 °C. ATK keeps finished batches on a rack in a **95 °C / 200 °F** oven.
- **Double fry (optional, extra crunch):** fry until cooked through, chill **at least 30 min–1 h** (or overnight), then re-fry at **about 200 °C / 400 °F for about 5 min**.
- **Dredge:** flour with some **cornstarch** and a little baking powder. Work 2–3 tbsp of the marinade into the flour to make craggy bits. **Rest the dredged pieces on a rack for at least 10 min before frying** so the coating sticks.
- **Why:** if you preheat too low, the oil falls below about 150 °C and the coating soaks up oil. If you hold it too hot, the crust burns before the bone-in meat cooks. Cornstarch limits gluten, so the crust is crisp, not tough. A rest hydrates the flour so the coating stays on.
- **Cue:** steady, vigorous bubbling. A bread cube sizzles with fine bubbles at once. The crust is deep golden brown.
- **Failure → fix:** if the chicken is greasy, the oil was too cold or the pot crowded, so fry smaller batches and let the oil recover between them. If the coating falls off, it wasn't rested or the chicken was moved too early. If the outside is dark and the inside raw, the oil was too hot for bone-in pieces, so finish them in the oven.
- **Sources:** ATK, "Fried Chicken 101" (heat to 350–375 °F, hold 300–325 °F; rest 10 min; 200 °F oven) https://www.americastestkitchen.com/how_tos/10883-fried-chicken-101 · ATK, "Shimmer, Deep-Fry, and Smoke" (deep-fry start 325–375 °F; drops to 250–325 °F; bread-cube test) https://www.americastestkitchen.com/articles/2977-shimmer-deep-fry-and-smoke-how-oil-behaves-at-different-temperatures · Serious Eats, "Buttermilk-Brined Southern Fried Chicken" (425 → 300 °F, oven finish, re-fry at 400 °F) https://www.seriouseats.com/the-food-lab-southern-fried-chicken-recipe · Serious Eats, "Four Secrets for Better Fried Chicken" (double fry; vodka 2 tbsp per cup of marinade; marinade in the flour) https://www.seriouseats.com/four-secrets-for-better-fried-chicken

## C7. Cutlets and schnitzel
- **Rule:**
  1. Split the breasts horizontally and pound them to an **even 6 mm (¼ in)**.
  2. Season, or brine briefly for **30 min** (Serious Eats).
  3. Bread with **flour → egg → crumbs**: shake off the flour, let the egg drip off so the layer is thin, and press on the crumbs. ATK adds **1 tbsp oil to 2 eggs**.
  4. Rest on a rack for **about 5 min**.
  5. Shallow-fry in **about 1 cm of oil at 175–190 °C (350–375 °F)**, 2–3 pieces at a time. Cook ATK's thin cutlets **1–1½ min a side**, shaking the pan gently so the hot oil washes over the top. Serious Eats cooks about 2 min a side.
  6. Drain on paper towel and hold in a **95 °C oven**.
- **Why:** an even thickness cooks through in the time the crumb takes to brown. The rest helps the coating cling. Shaking the pan makes the classic wrinkled, puffed coat that lifts away from the meat.
- **Cue:** the crumb is wrinkled, light golden brown and puffed. Thin cutlets are cooked through by the time both sides are golden. Check one at 74 °C.
- **Failure → fix:** if the crumb burns before the meat cooks, the cutlet is too thick or the oil too hot, so pound it thinner. If the coat is soggy, there was too much egg, the oil was too cold, or the pieces were stacked after frying, so blot them on paper and keep them in a single layer.
- **Sources:** ATK, "Chicken Schnitzel" https://www.americastestkitchen.com/recipes/12371-chicken-schnitzel · Serious Eats, "Chicken Schnitzel" https://www.seriouseats.com/chicken-schnitzel-recipe

## C8. Whole roast chicken
- **Rule:** dry brine (C2). Truss, or at least tuck the wings, then roast hot, **about 220 °C / 425 °F**. Roast until the **breast and the thigh both read ≥ 74 °C**, then rest **10–20 min** before carving.
  - **Pépin:** a 1.6 kg bird roasted at 220 °C in a cast iron skillet, **20 min on one side, 20 min on the other, then 10 min breast-up**, basting with the pan juices. Roasting on the sides protects the breast and needs little basting.
  - **Serious Eats:** a 1.8–2.3 kg bird. Brown each leg side in a skillet for **8–10 min**, then turn breast-up into a 220 °C oven for **about 40 min**.
- **Why:** the legs need more heat than the breast. Starting leg-side down, or roasting on the sides, gets the thighs to temperature before the breast dries out. Trussing makes the bird compact and easier to carve.
- **Cue:** the skin is deep golden and taut. The juices from the thigh joint run clear, but always confirm with a probe.
- **Failure → fix:** if the breast is dry and the thighs are underdone, roast leg-side first or on the sides, as above. If the skin is pale, the bird wasn't dried, the oven was too low, or the pan was too deep and crowded with vegetables, which make steam.
- **Sources:** Jacques Pépin, "Basic Roast Chicken" (NYT Cooking) https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/11353-jacques-pepins-basic-roast-chicken · Serious Eats, "Perfect Roast Chicken" https://www.seriouseats.com/perfect-roast-chicken-recipe-8384377 · Jacques Pépin, KQED Essential Pépin, "How to Truss a Chicken for Roasting" [V] (trussing holds the bird together for easier carving) https://www.kqed.org/jpepinheart/780/jacques-pepin-teaches-you-how-to-truss-a-chicken-for-roasting · Serious Eats, resting science https://www.seriouseats.com/meat-resting-science-11776272

## C9. Curry base (onion, aromatics, spices, liquid)
- **Rule (sequence):**
  1. Heat the fat (oil, butter or ghee). **Whole spices** (cumin seed, cinnamon, cardamom) go in first, for **10 s–2 min**, until fragrant.
  2. Cook the **onions until golden**. Light caramel at the edges takes **4–5 min** over medium-high (Iyer). Deep golden takes **15–20 min** over medium (Sodha). Jaffrey browns the chicken first in the spiced oil, then sautés the onions until they brown lightly at the edges.
  3. Add the **ginger-garlic(-chilli) paste** and cook **about 2 min** (Sodha). Jaffrey stirs chopped garlic in for a few seconds.
  4. Add the **ground spices** and fry **about 10 s**, stirring once or twice in the fat (Iyer, Jaffrey). **House rule: fry ground spices in fat before any liquid goes in.** (Sodha adds hers with the tomato paste after the tomatoes. Iyer and Jaffrey fry them in the fat first. We follow Iyer and Jaffrey because they get more aroma and give a clearer cue.)
  5. Add **tomato** and cook until the oil separates at the edges (**5–7 min**).
  6. **Add the yogurt slowly**, whisking it in (Sodha), so it doesn't split.
  7. Add the chicken and liquid. Simmer covered: **12–15 min** for breast chunks in sauce, **about 30 min** for thigh chunks, **about 15 min covered, then reduce uncovered** for bone-in pieces (Jaffrey).
  8. Add the **garam masala at the end** (the last 5 min).
- **Why:** browned onion gives sweetness and body. Many spice aromas are fat-soluble, and frying them develops flavour that simmering in water can't. Adding yogurt slowly keeps its proteins from curdling. Garam masala's aromatics are volatile, so late addition keeps them bright.
- **Cue:** the sauce darkens and the oil "dots" or separates at the edges after the tomato. The raw onion smell is gone and the sauce is thick enough to coat a spoon. The chicken reads 74 °C.
- **Failure → fix:** if the curry is thin or raw-tasting, the onions were under-browned or the spices were added to liquid. If the yogurt is grainy or split, it went in all at once or boiled hard. If the spices are burnt and bitter, ground spices went into very hot, dry fat for too long, so take the pan off the heat or add the tomato at once.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats / Raghavan Iyer, "Ultimate Chicken Curry (Tamatar Murghi)" https://www.seriouseats.com/raghavan-iyer-ultimate-chicken-curry-from-indian-cooking-unfolded · NYT Cooking, "Meera Sodha's Chicken Curry" https://cooking.nytimes.com/recipes/1017746-meera-sodhas-chicken-curry · Madhur Jaffrey, "Chicken Curry with Cardamom" from *Curry Easy* (published by her publisher Penguin on The Happy Foodie) https://thehappyfoodie.co.uk/recipes/chicken-curry-with-cardamom/

## C10. Buttermilk marinade and fried-chicken seasoning
- **Rule:** buttermilk (+ 1 egg) + salt about **1 tbsp Diamond kosher per 2 cups (480 ml) buttermilk** + spices. Marinate **4 h to overnight**, refrigerated. Lift out and let the excess drip off before dredging.
- **Why:** salted buttermilk works as a brine. Its proteins help the flour coat stick and brown.
- **Sources:** Serious Eats, "Buttermilk-Brined Southern Fried Chicken" https://www.seriouseats.com/the-food-lab-southern-fried-chicken-recipe · Serious Eats, "Four Secrets for Better Fried Chicken" https://www.seriouseats.com/four-secrets-for-better-fried-chicken
